Industry overview · plain words · Oct 2026

How online weight-loss medication works, who sells it, who pays, and what's popular

How it works

Four ways people get GLP-1s. HealthAccess's partners are route 1.

A prescription is needed for every legal route. But on most online clinics, the buyer never meets the doctor.

1 · Online clinicForm → clinician approves → pharmacy mails it

Most of this market, and every HealthAccess partner. Usually no call or video: a licensed clinician reviews the form behind the scenes, often the same day. Some states require a video or phone visit first.

Who meets a doctor: usually no one. The clinician works behind the scenes.

2 · Own doctorDoctor's visit → local pharmacy

The family doctor or a weight clinic prescribes. Usually the insurance route, with a prior-authorization request to the insurer.

Who meets a doctor: yes, in person or by video.

3 · Straight from the makerPrescription → LillyDirect or NovoCare

The drug company sells brand-name drugs without insurance at a set price. The prescription still comes from route 1 or 2. LillyDirect links to partner online clinics.

Who meets a doctor: depends on where the prescription came from.

4 · Gray marketOrder online → no doctor at all

"Research peptides" sold "not for human use", with no prescription. Cheapest ($10–$35 a month) and unsafe. of comments that name a drug are about this.

Who meets a doctor: nobody.

Route 1 step by step: how an online clinic works

  1. SearchFor example "Wegovy price". A comparison site like HealthAccess shows a list of providers.
  2. Pick a providerThe buyer clicks through. The comparison site is usually paid per sign-up or per lead.
  3. Pay and fill in a formWeight, health history, sometimes a photo or ID. Many providers charge at checkout, before approval.
  4. Clinician approvesA doctor or nurse reviews the form, usually without a call, and prescribes or declines, often within hours.
  5. Pharmacy shipsUsually within 2–7 days. Compounded comes from a compounding pharmacy; brand-name from a mail-order or local pharmacy.
  6. RefillsA short check-in form, then the next shipment. The dose goes up step by step. Prepaid plans may ship several months at once.

Why this matters: since most buyers never meet anyone, "a human who answers" is the top need in the data (). Most problems happen at steps 5 and 6: late refills, surprise charges, hard to cancel. Most star ratings are collected at steps 3 and 4.

The drugs

GLP-1 drugs reduce hunger. There are two main medicines and several brand names.

GLP-1 is a hormone the body makes after eating. These drugs copy it, so people feel full sooner and think about food less. Most are a weekly injection; two are daily pills.

Why compounded exists2022–2024: Wegovy and Zepbound ran short, so the FDA let pharmacies make copies. Online clinics built a business on them at $99–$299, when brand cost $1,000+.

Who pays

Almost everyone pays out of pocket. Insurance is the exception.

Out of pocket means the buyer pays the full price themselves, by card, without insurance. In the industry this is often called "cash pay".

of buyers who say how they pay are paying out of pocket

Why so many?

  • Insurance never covers compounded drugs, and most buyers in the data are on compounded.
  • Insurance often refuses brand-name drugs for weight loss. About 62% of claims were rejected (2021–24), and some employers dropped coverage in 2026.
  • Even with insurance it's hard to afford: 56% of GLP-1 users say so, including 55% of those with insurance (KFF, Nov 2025).

For a comparison site, this means the price people see is the price they pay. The out-of-pocket monthly price has to be the main number on every card, with a separate route for the insured and Medicare minority.

Glossary

The words you'll hear in the meeting

Out of pocket / cash pay
Paying the full price yourself, without insurance.
GLP-1
The family of weight-loss drugs that reduce hunger.
Brand-name
The original, FDA-approved drug: Wegovy, Zepbound, Foundayo.
Compounded
The same ingredient mixed by a specialist pharmacy. Cheaper, not FDA-approved.
Telehealth
An online clinic: health form or video visit, prescription, home delivery.
Prior authorization
When the doctor must ask the insurer for approval before it pays. Often denied for weight loss.
Membership
A monthly fee to the clinic, separate from the drug.
Dose / titration
Starting low and raising the dose every few weeks to limit side effects.
Maintenance dose
The dose people stay on long-term. The fair price to compare.
503A / 503B
Two types of compounding pharmacy. 503B makes larger batches under stricter rules.
Prepay / bundle
Paying several months upfront for a discount.
Research peptides
Unapproved drugs sold "not for human use". The gray market.

          How to test it on any comparison page

          • Hero test: drug-led ("Get Wegovy online") vs fear-led ("One all-in price. Cancel anytime. Real people.")
          • Card test: "from $X" vs all-in monthly price as the headline number. Measure click-outs and partner approvals.

          Seven findings across 701 reviews

          1. The star rating depends on when it's collected. Each provider asks at its best moment, and none ask at month 3. Invited reviews score 2–2.7★ higher than the rest.
          2. Reps ask for reviews that name them. One SkinnyRx review is the rep's own script: "Please be sure to mention my name."
          3. Each provider breaks at a different stage, and each stage maps to one field in the table.
          4. Prepaid bundles are the biggest money risk. 4 of 6 providers advertise their lowest price on 3–12-month prepay, and refunds are refused when they fail.
          5. The membership surprise comes from the business model: both Ro and Mochi get it.
          6. "Doesn't work" complaints cluster around compounded products, especially oral tablets.
          7. Switching is constant: membership fees push buyers to flat-price providers, failures push them back, and transferred doses are often not honoured.

          What this means for any comparison site

          • Don't show raw Trustpilot stars. They send people to providers that fail in month 2, and the refunds, chargebacks and brand damage come back to the site that sent them. Show a real customer score that separates invited from unprompted reviews.
          • Measure refill reliability. Every provider gets the first shipment out fast; month 2 is where they fail. No competitor tracks this. It can be a column in the comparison table.
          • Protect people switching providers. Cheap compounded offers pull people away from LillyDirect and other providers, then don't honour their dose. Several went back and paid more.
          • Judge partners on month-2 revenue. If Embody is a partner, ask for refund and chargeback data. Earnings per click should use retained customers, not first sign-ups.
          • Use "felt heard, not judged" in trust copy only for providers that actually deliver it.

          Dec 2025 →

          Brand drugs got cheaper for people paying out of pocket

          Where we can win
          • Show the real cost. Every page quotes a "from $X" price. Nobody shows the all-in monthly cost at the dose people stay on, which is the top complaint in reviews.
          • Route insured and out-of-pocket buyers separately. Insured, Medicare and out-of-pocket buyers want different providers. Nobody asks which one you are.
          • Give honest trust signals. Label every option FDA-approved or compounded, date-stamp prices, and flag regulatory issues. Raw Trustpilot stars favour the riskiest sellers.
          • Design for mobile from the start. Stacked cards, filter chips and a sticky button, not a shrunken desktop table.
          Rule that shapes every paid comparison page

          Since Oct 29, 2025, Google lets ads mention drug names, but bidding on drug-name keywords (semaglutide, Wegovy, Zepbound, Foundayo) needs Google healthcare certification. LegitScript certifies price-comparison sites. Whether a comparison site qualifies decides which half of the keyword universe it can buy.

          1. Google Ads search terms report, last 90 days, with cost and conversions. Shows what users actually look for.
          2. Keyword and ad group structure, with the landing page for each ad group and Quality Score parts.
          3. Partner economics: payout model and amount per partner, and partner-confirmed conversions (earnings per click-out).
          4. On-site funnel by device: landing, scroll, click-out, by list position and CTA type.
          5. Heatmaps and recordings (Hotjar or Clarity), plus past A/B test results.
          6. Full-page screenshots of every landing page variant, desktop and mobile.
          7. Their own customer data (quiz answers, support tickets, post-click surveys) to check the buyer profile against real HealthAccess users.

          Open questions for the client

          • Is the site certified (Google healthcare or LegitScript) to bid on drug-name keywords?
          • Which lane: brand-only, compounded, or both shown and clearly split?
          • What conversion is imported into Google Ads: click-out, lead, or partner sale?
          • Do partner contracts forbid bidding on their brand terms?
          • Can partners send back approved and started events, split by device?
          • Who reviews medical claims and compliance copy before a test goes live?
